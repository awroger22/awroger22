Sportswashing and Its Effectiveness: How the 2022 FIFA World Cup Shaped Qatar’s International Image

Author

Asa Rogers

Published

October 7, 2026

Introduction

There have been few events recently that have blurred the line between sports and geopolitics like the 2022 FIFA World Cup in Qatar did. For decades, observers have discussed whether (and how) autocratic governments can and do use mega-events to launder their international reputations, with that practice coming to be known as “sportswashing.” The Qatar World Cup has pushed that discussion into the global spotlight in an unusually direct way. The billions of dollars in infrastructure investment, a migrant labor system that drew widespread condemnation, and a host nation with a human rights record under sustained international scrutiny all collided with the biggest sporting event on earth.

But for many viewers, the political outrage that dominated the lead-up to the tournament seemed to disappear as the matches got underway. This tension between what audiences knew about Qatar and what they felt watching the football is the central puzzle I set out to review.

Why I Chose This Topic

I mainly came to this topic because I noticed that tension in myself. Going into the tournament, I was fully aware of the controversies surrounding Qatar: the questions about corruption in the bidding process, the treatment of the migrant workers who built the stadiums, and the restrictions on LGBTQ+ expression. I even questioned whether I should boycott the tournament altogether.

My love for watching soccer won out, and four years later, I find myself thinking about Qatar pretty neutrally, maybe even with a flicker of something positive when I recall the tournament itself. That subtle, almost involuntary shift in my own perception made me want to investigate whether the same thing happened at a larger scale, and what it might mean for how autocratic states use sport to shape the way the world sees them.

The Five Sources

To explore these questions, I gathered five sources that approach the effectiveness of sportswashing from different methodological angles:

  • Gläßel, Scharpf, and Edwards (2025) surveyed Germans immediately around the opening ceremony, providing the most direct short-term test of whether the tournament moved public opinion.
  • Gerschewski and colleagues (2024) ran a larger experiment across eight European countries, revealing how the media environment a person lives in shapes their susceptibility to image management.
  • Griffin (2019) provides essential context on Qatar’s intent, arguing that the World Cup was a deliberate nation-building project.
  • Al-Emadi and colleagues (2025) offer the only perspective rooted inside Qatar itself, examining how the country’s own residents felt about hosting the event.
  • Hassan and Wang (2024) analyzed over eight million tweets from the opening ceremony through the final, tracking the real-time emotional arc of global discourse as the competition progressed.

What This Review Covers

This literature review examines how the 2022 FIFA World Cup functioned as a sportswashing strategy for Qatar by analyzing:

  • the short-term effects of the tournament on international public opinion
  • the role of national media environments in shaping susceptibility to Qatar’s image management
  • the deliberate nation-building intent behind Qatar’s hosting strategy
  • the real-time sentiment arc on social media during the tournament
  • the attitudes of Qatar’s own resident population

By synthesizing findings from survey experiments, Twitter analysis, and cultural scholarship, this review aims to clarify whether and how mega-events can meaningfully shift how autocratic regimes are perceived abroad.

Short-Term Indirect Effects on Public Opinion

Researchers examining the immediate impact of the 2022 World Cup on public opinion find that the tournament did not dramatically improve Qatar’s direct international image, but it produced measurable indirect effects suggesting sportswashing operates through subtler channels than simple image rehabilitation.

A German Survey Before and After the Opening Ceremony

Gläßel et al. (2025) surveyed a sample of Germans twice, once in the weeks before the World Cup and again immediately after the opening ceremony and first matches. They deliberately timed the second survey before Germany’s own team played in order to isolate Qatar’s effect from any national pride or disappointment.

Their central finding was that Germans’ direct opinions of Qatar as a nation did not improve, but two indirect effects emerged clearly. Respondents showed significantly more distrust of German media’s reporting on Qatar, and they rated Arab nations more positively overall.

This distinction matters because sportswashing may not need to make audiences admire a country. It may only need to erode confidence in the sources that sustain criticism and generate a diffuse regional goodwill that is harder to trace back to any single political judgment.

An Eight-Country Experiment

Gerschewski et al. (2024) reach broadly similar conclusions to Gläßel et al. through a larger-scale experimental design. They surveyed over 14,000 respondents across eight European countries and randomly assigned participants to neutral, negative (human rights), or positive (efficiency and organization) framing conditions.

Framing did shift attitudes: positive framing improved opinions of Qatar, and negative framing worsened them. However, the net effect of the tournament across the full sample was modest and uneven. Countries with more pluralistic media environments saw weaker sportswashing gains, while countries where critical coverage was less prominent showed greater susceptibility to Qatar’s messaging.

Together, these studies suggest that the World Cup’s short-term effect on opinion was real but indirect, operating less by changing minds than by quietly reshaping the informational environment in which those minds were working and looking.

Media Environment

One of the most consistent findings across the literature is that the effectiveness of sportswashing is not uniform. It depends heavily on the media landscape of the country receiving Qatar’s messaging.

Weaker Press Pluralism, Greater Susceptibility

Gerschewski et al. (2024) provide the most direct evidence for this, demonstrating that the human rights framing in their experiment was significantly less effective at countering sportswashing in countries with weaker press pluralism. Where critical journalism is strong and diverse, audiences encounter more resistance to image management. Where it is weaker or more concentrated, Qatar’s positive framing faces less competition.

Why Strong Media Is Not a Complete Defense

Gläßel et al. (2025) add an important nuance to this picture. Their German sample, drawn from one of the countries Gerschewski et al. identify as having strong media pluralism, showed that the World Cup actually increased distrust in German media’s reporting on Qatar specifically, even while leaving respondents’ direct opinions unchanged. This suggests a more complicated dynamic: even in environments with robust critical coverage, the spectacle of the tournament may undermine the authority of that coverage, seeding doubt about whether media criticism of the host nation was politically motivated or journalistically reliable.

Hassan and Wang’s (2024) Twitter analysis complements this finding from a different angle. Across the tournament, they found that celebrity endorsements and the organic excitement of sporting results consistently outpaced political and media framing in shaping sentiment online. Even when critical voices were present, they were drowned out not by counter-messaging but by the sheer emotional volume of the football itself.

Taken together, these findings suggest that media environments shape how much room sportswashing has to work, but that the feel-good energy of a mega-event can erode that protection even in relatively media-literate societies.

Qatar’s Nation-Building Strategy

Understanding whether sportswashing “worked” requires first understanding what Qatar was actually trying to do, and Griffin (2019) provides the most thorough account of that intent.

Image Reconstruction as Strategy

Griffin writes right before the tournament’s opening that Qatar’s bid for and preparation of the 2022 World Cup was not just a prestige project but a deliberate strategy of international image reconstruction, designed to position Qatar as a legitimate global actor distinct from both Western stereotypes and its Gulf neighbors.

Griffin traces how Qatar used the World Cup to challenge Western narratives about Arab culture, to showcase government reforms in migrant labor policy and women’s sport, and to craft a national brand that could speak simultaneously to domestic pride and international credibility. These effects were already visible before the first match was played, with labor laws being revised, women’s sport receiving increased institutional support, and Qatar’s national imagery being substantially rebranded for international audiences.

Inside Qatar: How Residents Saw the Tournament

Al-Emadi et al. (2025) extend this picture inward, examining how Qatar’s own population related to the tournament that their government was using as a vehicle for international image-building. They surveyed 2,031 Qatar residents in 2019, split evenly between Qatari nationals and non-Qatari expatriates. Support for the tournament was widespread but varied significantly by demographic: males, non-Qatari residents, and more highly educated respondents showed the greatest enthusiasm and the highest likelihood of attending matches.

A population that was broadly (though unevenly) enthusiastic about the tournament was itself part of the image that the government was projecting outward.

Traction With Outside Audiences

Gerschewski et al. (2024) add another layer by showing that positive framing about Qatar’s hosting capacity and organizational efficiency moved attitudes noticeably. This suggests that Qatar’s deliberate effort to present the tournament as a showcase of national pride had real traction with outside audiences.

Across these three sources, it is clear that the World Cup was a premeditated image project, executed with clear intent and at least partial success.

Conclusion

What the Evidence Shows

The five sources reviewed here, together, offer a complex and coherent answer to the question of how effective the 2022 World Cup was as a sportswashing strategy for Qatar. Scholars largely agree that the tournament did not dramatically rehabilitate Qatar’s image overnight: direct opinions about the country as a political actor barely changed, and the human rights criticisms that defined the lead-up did not really disappear.

But it is equally clear that the World Cup produced subtler and quieter shifts that may ultimately prove more consequential, especially long-term. Trust in critical media coverage of Qatar abroad eroded, and goodwill toward Arab nations noticeably increased. And the organic excitement of the competition displaced the political controversies that dominated pre-tournament narratives on social media, demonstrating measurable susceptibility to positive framing about Qatar’s organizational competence. All of this suggests (at least in this case) that sportswashing works less like direct persuasion and more like slow erosion.

What Remains Unknown

What remains underexplored is whether these effects lasted, as none of the quantitative studies in this review can speak to long-term effects, and the lack of non-Western perspectives leaves a portion of the global audience unaccounted for. Future research should prioritize long-term tracking of attitudes in the years following the tournament and compare work across other recent mega-events used for image management.

Why It Matters

At a moment when sportswashing is accelerating globally, the stakes of understanding how and when it works extend well beyond the academic. They touch on who gets to shine on the world’s biggest stages, whose stories get told in the process, and whether the scrutiny those events generate is powerful enough to outlast the spectacle.
